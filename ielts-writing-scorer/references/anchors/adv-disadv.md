A subscription is not one kind of transaction, and whether its spread is positive depends on which kind is spreading. Sometimes the fee pays for something the seller goes on providing: updates, repairs, a replacement when the model wears out. Where a subscription buys a continuing service, it is often the better arrangement. A family that rents a cot for the months a baby needs it, then returns it for someone else to use, spends less and discards less than one that buys. Spreading the cost also puts equipment within reach of households that could never buy it outright.

Where it merely rents out an unchanging object, the arrangement works against the customer in three ways. It costs more over any reasonable lifetime, because it is priced on how long the customer can be kept paying rather than on what the object cost to make. It transfers control: once a household's car or heating depends on a monthly payment, the seller can raise the price, withdraw a feature or rewrite the terms, and the customer's only protest is to walk away from everything already paid. And it profits partly from inattention, since a subscription forgotten is revenue collected for nothing, which is why leaving is so often made harder than joining.

I therefore regard the trend as negative, not because subscriptions are inherently bad but because their growth is concentrated where they offer least. The test is simple.
