**全文**

The distinction the question draws is doing more work than it can bear, so I would support the reform while rejecting the line it has been drawn on. What determines whether custody helps is not whether force was used but whether the sentence has anything to accomplish that liberty would prevent.

For most low-level acquisitive offending it does not. Three months removes a person from a job they will not get back, a tenancy that will not be held open and a family that absorbs the cost of their absence, and returns them with a criminal record and a weakened set of reasons to stay out of trouble. Nothing in those three months touches the addiction, the debt or the chaotic housing that produced the offence, because nothing worth starting can be finished in twelve weeks. The sentence is not too harsh or too soft. It is the wrong tool, and its main measurable achievement is to make the next offence more likely than it was.

Community disposals can be the right tool, but only where they are actually resourced. An unpaid work requirement with no treatment attached, supervised by a probation officer carrying eighty other cases, is not a sentence, and the public is right to notice. This is where the argument is usually lost — not on principle, but because underfunded alternatives fail visibly and prison then looks like the responsible choice.

Where I part from the proposal is over its category. A fraud that empties a pension scheme is non-violent, and there is no serious case for a community order: the harm is enormous, the offender is rarely chaotic, and deterrence in that population responds to precisely one thing. A scuffle outside a pub, meanwhile, is violent and may well be better answered outside prison.

So the reform is right and its criterion is wrong. Sentence according to the harm done and to what a sentence can realistically achieve, rather than according to whether anybody was hit.
