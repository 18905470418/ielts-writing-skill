**全文**

Fraud committed over the internet has grown faster than almost any other category of crime. The reasons are structural rather than accidental, and while individuals cannot fix the structure, there is a good deal they can do to make themselves harder to reach.

The first reason is that the amount of exploitable information has multiplied. Shopping, banking, tax returns and social life have all moved online, so a single compromised account can expose an address, a date of birth and a list of contacts at once. Criminals no longer need to work out who a target is; the information has been published, often by the target.

The second is that the crime is unusually low-risk. An offender can operate from a jurisdiction that will not extradite, against victims whose police have no authority there, using intermediaries to move the proceeds. Investigations crossing several borders are slow, and most individual losses are too small to justify them, so the expected cost of committing this crime is close to zero. Automation compounds the problem, since a message costs nothing to send to a million people and needs to succeed only occasionally.

Individual defence therefore has to concentrate on being an unprofitable target. The single most valuable measure is two-factor authentication, which makes a stolen password useless on its own. A password manager removes the temptation to reuse credentials across sites. Beyond the technical measures, the habit that matters most is refusing to act on urgency: almost every successful scam depends on persuading somebody to move money or reveal a code immediately, so a rule of never responding to unexpected contact directly, and instead calling the organisation back on a number found independently, defeats most attempts.

None of this eliminates the risk, but fraud at this scale is a numbers business, and anyone slightly harder to defraud than average is likely to be passed over.
