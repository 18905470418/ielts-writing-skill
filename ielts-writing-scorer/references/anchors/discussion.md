**全文**

When faced with difficult circumstances, such as an unfulfilling job or financial hardship, opinions diverge sharply on the wisest course of action. Some maintain that acceptance is the more pragmatic response, while others insist that actively working to change one's situation is preferable. In my view, while acceptance has its place in certain contexts, deliberate efforts to improve one's circumstances generally yield far greater long-term benefits.

Proponents of acceptance argue, first and foremost, that it protects mental wellbeing. Constantly striving against circumstances that cannot easily be changed — a chronic illness, for instance, or a deeply unstable economy — can generate persistent stress and a corrosive sense of failure when improvement proves elusive. Philosophies such as Stoicism have long championed this view, holding that peace of mind stems not from controlling external events, but from mastering one's own response to them. There is also a pragmatic dimension: not every unsatisfactory situation is within an individual's power to resolve, and recognising this can prevent the exhaustion of energy on efforts that are ultimately futile.

Advocates of active change, however, contend that most difficulties, however entrenched, admit at least partial improvement, and that resignation forecloses possibilities that effort might otherwise realise. A person trapped in an unsatisfactory job, for example, can pursue further qualifications, seek a transfer, or actively search for alternative employment; someone facing financial hardship can budget more carefully, acquire new skills, or seek additional income streams. Historical and contemporary examples abound of individuals who transformed dire circumstances through sustained effort rather than passive endurance — entrepreneurs who built successful enterprises from poverty, or workers who retrained entirely to escape declining industries. To accept such situations uncritically, this view holds, is to squander agency that could otherwise be exercised productively.

On balance, I find the case for active improvement more persuasive, though not unconditionally so. The distinction, I believe, lies in whether a circumstance is genuinely immutable or merely difficult. Where a situation can plausibly be improved through reasonable effort, resignation often reflects avoidance rather than wisdom, and forfeits the chance of a better outcome. Where a circumstance is truly beyond one's control, however, acceptance is not merely sensible but necessary, since persistent struggle against the immovable serves only to compound suffering. The wisdom, then, lies not in choosing acceptance or effort as a universal principle, but in accurately judging which applies to a given situation.

In conclusion, both acceptance and active improvement have legitimate roles depending on circumstance, but I believe that where meaningful change remains genuinely possible, the effort to pursue it is almost always the more constructive path. Passive endurance should be reserved for what truly cannot be altered, not adopted as a default response to difficulty.

The resolution I would propose is a sequenced one: a common foundation in the early secondary years that ensures every student acquires the cognitive tools and cultural literacy that adult life requires, followed by a progressively expanding zone of elective study as students mature. Breadth enables depth; the foundations of genuine specialisation are often laid in subjects one would not have chosen for oneself.
